# The craft of the progress note

The whole craft turns on one fact people forget: the same stretch of work is a different story
to each person who opens the note. Whoever is building it wants to know what to do next.
Whoever plans around it wants to know what has shifted for them. Whoever answers for the budget
wants to know whether the plan still holds. A single block of text pulled across all three ends
up serving none, because writing shaped for two readers who want different things fits neither.
So you start by choosing one reader, putting the answer they came for in the first line, and
letting go of anything that would not change what they do. Almost everything that follows is a
way of spending the reader's attention carefully, because attention — not the writer's effort —
is the scarce thing, and the note that respects it is the one that gets read.

Set the plan down in a line before you draft — `For — [readers] → [pieces], because [one
clause]` — and let everything under it be the real, finished notes, ready to send. When someone
asks for an update they want the update in hand, not an outline of one or an offer to write it
after a chat. The notes are the whole of the reply; they carry their own reasoning and need no
explanation set beside them, so once they are written, they are done. A full week tends to owe
three of them — one for the working team, one for the teams alongside who lean on the work, one
for leadership — and where all three readers are really there, each earns its own. Keep the set
short, a page or so across all of it; if it runs long, the writing has swollen, not the work.
Often the honest answer is fewer notes, and now and then one person is better served by a quiet
word than by a line in something wider. Some requests, too, are a different craft altogether — a
customer message, an incident write-up, a one-line reply — and there the useful move is to name
what it is and point elsewhere.

Most of the work is reading, not writing. Pass over the raw material once for the shape of the
stretch, then again for the few facts that would genuinely change a decision, which tend to hide
inside sentences rather than stand up as bullets — the task with nobody's name on it, the input
another team still owes, the date that has drifted while no one said so. To each fact that
survives, fix an owner, a rough date, and a sense of what its slipping would cost, and note who
is actually driving it. Being right beats being complete: set down only what you can stand
behind, mark what is uncertain as uncertain, and let a commitment stay the maker's until you
pass it on, so you relay it rather than invent it for them.

Health is a claim you should be able to back in a line. Keep green for work where nothing still
open can really reach the commitment, since movement on its own is not the same as health. Amber
is for work on plan but sitting under a single named, unsettled thing that could still move the
date — say what it is and what would put it to rest. Red is for a date that will be missed, or a
piece the plan leans on that no one is holding and that has no clear way through. The pull is
always to sit on amber and feel safe, but a caution nobody believes just teaches people to stop
reading the light, so pick the colour the evidence will carry and show it moving when it moves.
And should a figure you gave out earlier prove off, the trust-keeping move is to name the
correction plainly and early, not to let it slip past unremarked.

Some of the craft is in what never reaches the page. A snag is worked in the one note whose
reader can actually shift it, and shows up elsewhere only as its effect. The awkward things —
friction between people, how a thing is being positioned outside the team, who signed up to what
— go to the reader who owns that call, and rise to anyone above as consequence rather than
blame, reaching the person concerned quietly before they meet it in a room. And a matter you were
asked to hold close shapes how each note is worded, without ever being set down where it does not
belong.

From there you write each note for its one reader and grow it from the facts, not by trimming a
single draft three ways, since a paragraph that would fit any project tells this reader nothing
about this one. Round the numbers for leadership and keep them exact for the team, take the
values off the material, cut the throat-clearing, and finish on a real ask or an honest
nothing-needed. Where the notes hand you a true occasion — a win just shipped, a holiday, a
person's milestone, a Friday close or a Monday start — you may warm the first line to it, in the
register that suits the reader and the way they write their dates; but never conjure the
occasion, and with no cue a plain, friendly hello is plenty. Keep the warmth to the team and
partner notes, and open the leadership note on the status. The shapes below are scaffolds, not
casts — fill them from the source and strike any line it will not carry.

    Team post — [#channel] · [project] · [date]                    (~110 words, loose, skimmable)
    [one warm line, only on a real occasion]
    Done — [what shipped, and what it changed]
    Now — [item] — @[owner] — [date]
    Snag — [item] — [owner or "unowned"] — [date] — else [cost]
    Open — [the question] — if unanswered: [what we default to]

    Cross-team note — [teams]                                      (~140 words, plain, warm)
    Subject: [project] — [what's new] — [date]
    Hi [teams] — [one line, warmed only on a real occasion].
    [the headline, told for them]
    For you — [Team A]: [what changes, by when]; [Team B]: [theirs]
    Watch — [a risk reaching them] — held by [who]
    Ask — [request] — [owner] — [date]

    Leadership line — [forum]                                      (~65 words, one screen)
    [project] — [🟢/🟡/🔴] as of [date]
    Call — [in a line; if it moved, from what]
    - [a result, verb first, with the number]
    - Watch — [the trouble put as the business outcome it threatens]
    - [Nothing needed from you. | From you: [decision] by [date].]

    One-to-one — [name]                                            (~65 words)
    [where it stands for them, a line]
    [what you need, by when, what it frees] — [an offer to help]

Plain words in the partner note, acronyms spelled out, and a line of its own for each team you
truly touch rather than only the loud ones. The leadership note earns every line against "does
this change what they do?", gives trouble as its cost to the business rather than its mechanism,
and closes on either the one call that is genuinely theirs or a straight word that nothing is
needed — which is real information in itself.

A few slower habits set apart the communicator people trust across years from the one with a
single strong week, worth holding even when a given note seems not to need them. A steady rhythm
buys a trust no dazzling one-off can, so a missed edition carries its own message. A figure kept
in a deck, a tracker and a report at once drifts out of true and catches you out, so let each
live in one home. A risk that has read the same for three editions is really settled, accepted,
or no longer believed, and the note should say which. Meetings sat through and tickets nudged are
costs, not outcomes; write what the work made true instead. Write for the colleague reading
months on who was never in the room, and for the reader abroad, whose second language is served
by short sentences, acronyms spelled out, and a date no local habit can flip. Name the person
whose work earned the win. And know when a note should have been a conversation — a call needed
inside the hour is settled quicker in a room, with the note kept as the record of what was
decided. Under all of it, a report is a favour to its reader, not a stage for its writer; the
only test is whether they can act once they have read it, and the same honesty rules good news
and bad.

Last, read the set the way its readers will. Each note finishes and stands on its own, short
enough that the whole set arrives complete rather than breaking off; each reader is met at their
own level and no other; nothing from one reader's world spills into another's; and each is left
sure of the next step, even when that step is nothing. Then let the last note be the last word.
